## Quick answer

The leach field (also called the drainfield) is where your septic system finishes treating wastewater, by letting it soak slowly into the soil. When it is overloaded, clogged or damaged, wastewater has nowhere to go: drains slow down, sewage can back up into the house, and soggy ground or odors show up over the field. Not every leach field problem means replacement. A clogged filter, a broken pipe, a failed distribution box or too much water going into the system can all look like a failing field. A proper diagnosis comes first.

- Warning signs: slow drains throughout the house, gurgling, sewage odors, soggy ground or bright green grass over the field, and backups.
- Common causes: too much water, solids carried out of the tank, roots, crushed pipes and compacted soil.
- Some problems are repairs to one part. Others need a new or larger dispersal area.
- In Santa Cruz County, Environmental Health decides whether repair or upgrade standards apply.

## How a leach field works

According to the U.S. EPA, a typical septic system has two main parts: a septic tank and a drainfield. All the wastewater from the house flows into the tank, where solids settle to the bottom as sludge and oils and grease float to the top as scum. A T-shaped outlet keeps those layers in the tank.

The liquid, called effluent, then flows to the drainfield. In a conventional system that is a set of perforated pipes or chambers in shallow trenches in unsaturated soil. The soil accepts, treats and disperses the wastewater as it percolates down, removing harmful bacteria, viruses and nutrients before the water reaches groundwater.

EPA notes that if the drainfield is overloaded with too much liquid, it can flood, sending sewage to the ground surface or causing backups in toilets and sinks. That is the failure most homeowners notice first.

Many Santa Cruz County properties, especially on steep or wooded lots in the mountains, use alternative systems with pumps, sand filters or other treatment units before the effluent reaches the dispersal area. The same idea applies: the soil has to be able to accept the water.

## Warning signs of leach field trouble

- **Slow drains everywhere.** One slow sink is usually a local clog. Every fixture draining slowly points farther down the line.
- **Gurgling** in drains or toilets.
- **Sewage odors** inside, near the tank or over the field.
- **Soggy ground, standing water or unusually lush grass** over the field, especially when it lingers after the rest of the yard has dried.
- **Backups** into a tub, shower or floor drain, often when the washing machine drains.
- **A septic alarm** on a system with a pump chamber.

## Repair or upgrade: how Santa Cruz County looks at it

In Santa Cruz County, onsite wastewater treatment systems (OWTS) are permitted through County Environmental Health, and the rules depend on what is already permitted on your parcel.

- **Repair** means replacing an old or malfunctioning system that was permitted and correctly sized. According to Environmental Health, repair standards apply when the house does not exceed what the system was permitted for, with a one time allowance of less than 500 square feet if no bedrooms are added.
- **Upgrade** standards apply when the home now has more bedrooms, more than 500 additional square feet or more structures than the permit allowed, or when the work adds dispersal area or treatment components to meet current standards or serve an accessory dwelling unit.
- **No permitted system** on record means the parcel has to meet current standards for new development.
- **Steep ground:** a technical report is required when a system or future expansion area is proposed on slopes over 30 percent.

You can look up your parcel's septic permit history through Environmental Health's online records. ## Protect the field you have

EPA's drainfield guidance comes down to a few habits:

- **Do not park or drive on the field.** Weight compacts the soil and can crush pipes.
- **Plant carefully.** Keep trees and large shrubs far enough away that roots stay out of the lines. A septic professional can tell you the right distance for your layout.
- **Keep water away.** Point roof drains, sump pumps and other drainage away from the field. EPA notes that excess water slows down or stops treatment.
- **Use water efficiently.** EPA says a single leaky or running toilet can add as much as 200 gallons a day, and doing all the laundry in one day can flood the drainfield. Spread loads across the week.
- **Mind the drains.** Only human waste and toilet paper belong in the toilet. Keep grease, wipes and chemicals out, and go easy on the garbage disposal.
- **Inspect on schedule.** EPA recommends an inspection at least every three years for a typical household system, and about once a year for systems with pumps or other mechanical parts.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a failing leach field be repaired, or does it always need replacing?

It depends on the cause. A clogged filter, failed distribution box or broken pipe can often be fixed without replacing the field. Soil that has stopped accepting water usually needs new or additional dispersal area.

### Why is the grass greener over my leach field?

Some extra growth is normal, but very lush grass, soggy ground or odors over the field can mean wastewater is reaching the surface. Have it checked.

### Do I need a permit to repair a leach field in Santa Cruz County?

Septic repairs and upgrades go through County Environmental Health. Whether repair or upgrade standards apply depends on what your existing system was permitted for.

### Will a lot of rain hurt my leach field?

Long periods of rain can saturate the soil so it accepts less water, which slows the whole system. Keeping drainage away from the field and cutting back on water use during storms helps.
